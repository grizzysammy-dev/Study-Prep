# 802.11 Wireless

The Wi-Fi standards table (band + speed), how channels work, the WEP→WPA2→WPA3 security evolution with 802.1X, and the deauth/management-frame weakness. I've confirmed the Wi-Fi 7 status for 2026 down below.

## Quick recall
- **Standards → Wi-Fi name:** 802.11**n** = Wi-Fi 4, **ac** = Wi-Fi 5, **ax** = Wi-Fi 6/6E, **be** = **Wi-Fi 7**.
- **Bands:** 2.4 GHz (range, congested) · 5 GHz (speed) · **6 GHz** (new, clean, Wi-Fi 6E/7 only).
- **2.4 GHz has only 3 non-overlapping channels: 1, 6, 11.**
- **Security ladder:** **WEP (broken)** → WPA/TKIP (deprecated) → **WPA2/AES-CCMP** → **WPA3/SAE** (current best practice).
- **Enterprise auth = 802.1X / EAP + RADIUS** (per-user). **Deauth works because legacy management frames are unencrypted, fixed by 802.11w PMF (mandatory in WPA3).**

## The physics of it
802.11 is the IEEE family for wireless LANs. Two dials define each standard: **which band(s)** it uses (2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz) and **how fast** it goes. Higher bands carry more data but travel shorter distances and punch through walls worse, and that trade-off is basically the whole story of Wi-Fi's evolution, running alongside a parallel evolution in **encryption** (WEP → WPA3) because the early crypto was broken. The band trade-off is just physics: low frequency (2.4 GHz) is a bass note, it rolls through walls and travels far but carries less, and the neighborhood is crowded; high frequency (6 GHz) is a treble note, huge bandwidth but short-range and easily stopped by a wall.

## Standards table

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 802.11 | - | 1997 | 2.4 GHz | 2 Mbps |
| **802.11a** | - | 1999 | 5 GHz | 54 Mbps |
| **802.11b** | - | 1999 | 2.4 GHz | 11 Mbps |
| **802.11g** | - | 2003 | 2.4 GHz | 54 Mbps |
| **802.11n** | **Wi-Fi 4** | 2009 | 2.4 & 5 GHz | ~600 Mbps (MIMO) |
| **802.11ac** | **Wi-Fi 5** | 2013 | 5 GHz | ~3.5 Gbps (wave 2 ~6.9 theoretical) |
| **802.11ax** | **Wi-Fi 6 / 6E** | 2019 / 2021 | 2.4 & 5 GHz (**6E adds 6 GHz**) | ~9.6 Gbps (OFDMA) |
| **802.11be** | **Wi-Fi 7** | 2024 (pub. 22 Jul 2025) | 2.4, 5 & 6 GHz | ~**46 Gbps** (320 MHz, 4096-QAM, MLO) |

### Wi-Fi 7 status (confirmed 2026)
**802.11be-2024 (Wi-Fi 7) is a ratified IEEE standard**, published 22 Jul 2025, with Wi-Fi Alliance certification live since Jan 2024 and hardware shipping. As of 2026 it's the current top standard, not a draft. Headline features: **320 MHz** channels, **4096-QAM**, and **Multi-Link Operation (MLO)**, where a device uses the 2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz bands **simultaneously** for throughput, latency, and reliability. Wi-Fi 6E and 7 are the ones that unlock the **6 GHz** band.

## Channels
- **2.4 GHz**: only **3 non-overlapping** 20 MHz channels, **1, 6, 11**. Crowded and interference-prone.
- **5 GHz**: many more channels, some of them **DFS** (Dynamic Frequency Selection, must yield to radar).
- **6 GHz**: wide, clean spectrum (up to **320 MHz** channels for Wi-Fi 6E/7).

## Security evolution (WEP to WPA3)
- **WEP**: **broken.** RC4 with a 24-bit IV that repeats, so the key is recoverable in minutes. **Never use.**
- **WPA**: interim fix using **TKIP** (still RC4-based), deprecated.
- **WPA2**: **AES-CCMP** (strong cipher). Modes: **WPA2-PSK** (Personal, a shared passphrase, offline-crackable if weak, think 4-way-handshake / PMKID capture) and **WPA2-Enterprise** (per-user auth via **802.1X/EAP + RADIUS**). And remember the 2017 **KRACK** handshake attack.
- **WPA3**: **current best practice.** **SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals / "Dragonfly")** replaces the PSK handshake, giving **forward secrecy** and resistance to offline dictionary attacks; **192-bit** Enterprise mode.
- **802.1X / EAP**: port-based network access control, the **Enterprise** auth framework (**EAP-TLS** with certificates = strongest). Pairs with **NAC** (see [Network Security Devices](Network%20Security%20Devices.md)).
- **Management frames / deauth**: legacy 802.11 management frames are **unencrypted**, which enables **deauthentication / disassociation** attacks (jamming, forced reconnect, handshake capture, evil-twin). **802.11w (Protected Management Frames, PMF)** fixes this and is **mandatory in WPA3.** Why it works: a deauth frame is unauthenticated, so anyone can forge the "you're disconnected" message and every client obeys, like yelling a fake fire-alarm that clears the building because nobody checks who pulled it. PMF signs those frames, so the forgeries get ignored.

## What graders zero in on
- **Map standard ↔ Wi-Fi number:** n=4, ac=5, ax=6/6E, be=7. And **ax=6E and be=7 are the ones on 6 GHz.**
- **Band cheat:** 2.4 GHz = range + congestion (**channels 1/6/11**); 5 GHz = speed; 6 GHz = new/clean.
- **WEP is broken.** If asked what to never use, it's WEP. **WPA3/SAE + PMF** is the current answer.
- **Why deauth works = unencrypted management frames**; the fix is **802.11w PMF**, mandatory in WPA3. That cause→fix pair is a favorite.
- **Personal vs Enterprise:** PSK (shared passphrase) vs **802.1X/EAP + RADIUS** (per-user). Enterprise ties into NAC.
- **Wi-Fi 7 = ratified in 2026**, not draft. Key features are **MLO + 6 GHz + 320 MHz + 4096-QAM.**
